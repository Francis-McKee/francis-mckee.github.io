Bondage in Freedom

<br>

This chapter continues the narrative of the spies from the previous chapter.

Because of the negative report of the ten spies (minus Caleb and Joshua):

> Numbers 14:1-2 - "And all the congregation lifted up their voice, and cried; and the people wept that night. And all the children of Israel murmured against Moses and against Aaron: and the whole congregation said unto them, Would God that we had died in the land of Egypt! or would God we had died in this wilderness!"

The people feared that they would not be able to take the land from the Canaanites, and that they would be killed. They murmured against Moses and Aaron, wanting to establish a new leader and return to Egypt (4).

In Numbers 13, we encountered the idea of Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, that the spies were not afraid of failure, they were afraid of success. They wanted to stay in the wilderness, where they had felt close to God and could devote all of their time to Him.

## Bondage in Freedom

Maimonides had a simpler reading, closer to the plain sense of the text: the people were not yet ready to assume the burdens and responsibilities of fighting a series of battles, establishing a functioning society, and all else that goes with freedom, independence, and nationhood.

Maimonides saw this view prefigured in the verse with which the exodus from Egypt begins:

> Exodus 13:17 (NASB) - "Now when Pharaoh had let the people go, God did not lead them by the way of the land of the Philistines, even though it was near; for God said, 'The people might change their minds when they see war, and return to Egypt.'"

Maimonides comments: "Here God led the people about, away from the direct route He had originally intended, because He feared that they might encounter hardships too great for their present strength. So He took them by a different route in order to achieve His original object."

Yet despite the detour, what God feared came to pass. The people had second thoughts, so much so that they wanted to appoint a new leader and return back to Egypt (4).

Maimonides adds: "It is a well known fact that travelling in the wilderness without physical comforts... produces courage, while the opposite produces faintheartedness [cowardice/timidity]."

People long deprived of their freedom grow used to their chains. The fact remains that some prefer servitude to the responsibilities of freedom.

Michael Walzer says there is "a kind of bondage in freedom: the bondage of law, obligation, and responsibility."

> Exodus 7:16 (NASB) - "And you [Moses] shall say to him [Pharaoh], 'The Lord, the God of the Hebrews, sent me to you, saying, Let My people go, **so that they may serve Me in the wilderness.** But behold, you have not listened up to now.'"

> Exodus 2:23 - "And they [the Egyptians] made their [the children of Israel] lives bitter with hard bondage, in morter, and in brick, and in all manner of service in the field: all their service, wherein they made them serve, was with rigour."

The word for "servant" of God and for "slave" of Pharaoh is the same in Hebrew: *avad*/*abad*. Freedom, like slavery, is hard work. The difference between the two is that their work under Pharaoh was forced through bondage under law, but their service to God was a willing response out of faith.

So why did God not simply give the Israelites the strength or self-confidence they needed to enter the land? Because it would have meant saying goodbye to human freedom, choice, and responsibility. God wants human beings to bring heaven down to earth, to construct a society of freedom - and how could He do that if, in order to bring it about, He had to deprive them of the very freedom that He wanted them to exercise and honor?

There are some things a parent may not do for a child if he or she wants the child to become an adult. There are some things even God must choose not to do for His people if He wants them to grow to maturity.

The fact is that this change, change in the hearts of people, takes time. The Lord's response to the complaints of the people makes more sense now:

> Numbers 14:30-31 - "By no means will you come into the land where I swore to settle you, except for Caleb the son of Jephunneh and Joshua the son of Nun. Your children, however, whom you said would become plunder—I will bring them in, and they will know the land which you have rejected."

The people were not condemned to permanent exile, they simply had to face the fact that their children would achieve what they themselves were not ready for.

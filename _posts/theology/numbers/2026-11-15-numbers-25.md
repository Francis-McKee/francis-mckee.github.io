A Covenant of Peace

<br>

> Numbers 25:1-3 (NASB) - "While Israel remained at Shittim, the people began to commit infidelity with the daughters of Moab. For they invited the people to the sacrifices of their gods, and the people ate and bowed down to their gods. So Israel became followers of Baal of Peor [or, Baal-peor], and the Lord was angry with Israel."

This was the first occasion the Israelites had real contact with the people who would become their neighbors. They were no longer in the desert, far from cities and civilization, they were approaching the Promised Land. That the people could fall so quickly was a frightening sign of how little they had learned about the nature of their mission as an exemplary people.

Worse, the sinners were not the usual suspects, the people who came out of Egypt. A generation had passed, and the Israelites were coming close to their destination. This was the new generation in which all the hopes of the future were invested. Yet they too fell in the first trial.

The Lord punishes them with death, saying to Moses: "Take all the leaders of the people and execute them in broad daylight before the Lord, so that the fierce anger of the Lord may turn away from Israel" (4). The Lord also sent a plague onto the people.

Then one of the Israelites came cavorting with a Midianite woman in the sight of Moses and the whole congregation. 

> Numbers 25:7-8 (NASB) - "When Phinehas [some translations call him Pinchas] the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, saw it, he rose up from the midst of the congregation and took a spear in his hand, and he went after the man of Israel into the inner room of the tent and pierced both of them, the man of Israel and the woman, through the abdomen."

So the Lord brought the plague to a halt, but the total number of people who died from it was 24,000.

Where was Moses? The rabbis were surely right to suggest that it was Moses' own background that rendered him powerless in the given situation, for he had himself married a Midianite woman, the daughter of one of their priests (see [Exodus 2]({{ '/blog/exodus/2' | relative_url }})). Any attempt on Moses' part to do what Phinehas did would have exposed him to the charge of hypocrisy, and made the situation worse, not better.

> Numbers 25:10-13 (NASB) - "Then the Lord spoke to Moses, saying, "Phinehas the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, has averted My wrath from the sons of Israel in that he was jealous with My jealousy among them, so that I did not destroy the sons of Israel in My jealousy. Therefore say, 'Behold, I am giving him My covenant of peace; and it shall be for him and for his descendants after him, a covenant of a permanent priesthood, because he was jealous for his God and made atonement for the sons of Israel.'""

Here the Lord tells Moses that Phinehas has done a good thing. If Phinehas had not killed the man and woman, this plague may have wiped out all the Israelites. The Lord rewards Phinehas with a special "covenant of peace", promising that Phinehas and his descendants will always be priests.

We are then told of the names of the Israelite man (Zimri) and Midianite woman (Cozbi) who were killed by Phinehas. Zimri's father was a chieftain (leader) in the tribe of Simeon. Cozbi's father was a chieftain of the Midianites. As a couple, their influence and worship of the Midianite god Baal-peor caused many Israelites to follow their lead. This is why the Lord ended the plague when this couple were killed.

> Numbers 25:16-18 (NASB) - "Then the Lord spoke to Moses, saying, 'Be hostile to the Midianites and attack them; for they have been hostile to you with their tricks, with which they have deceived you in the matter of Peor and in the matter of Cozbi, the daughter of the leader of Midian, their sister who was killed on the day of the plague because of Peor.'"

Only several chapters later (Numbers 31), in the course of the war the Israelites eventually waged against the Midianites, do we discover that the entire episode of sexual and spiritual betrayal in this chapter was planned and conceived by Balaam. No Israelite was present at any point in the story of Balaam from [Numbers 22]({{ '/blog/numbers/22' | relative_url }}) to [Numbers 24]({{ '/blog/numbers/23-24' | relative_url }}). They could not have known the danger they were in of being cursed by a non-Israelite prophet. They could not have known that God Himself had intervened to turn the curses into blessings.

When the text withholds a fact essential to understanding a passage and reveals it only later, it is forcing us to realize that events are not always what they seem. In this case, certain puzzling aspects of the Balaam story now become clear. We now understand why God was angry with Balaam for going along with the Moabites and Midianites despite the fact that He had given Balaam permission to do so. Evidently, God detected in Balaam's mind a persisting malevolence towards Israel, which eventually found expression in the plan to have the women seduce the Israelite men.

The real effect of concealing the information about Balaam is to focus attention on the Israelites. Had Balaam's name been mentioned in this chapter, we would have focused on him. It would have been his malice, his defiance of God's purposes that would have been the story. The text is signaling to us that it was not the story. This is not where God wants our attention to be directed. It was the Israelites who sinned.

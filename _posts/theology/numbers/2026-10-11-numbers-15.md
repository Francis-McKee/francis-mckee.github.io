Law and Narrative

<br>

This chapter contains a cluster of laws. One law speaks of the *challah* offering - setting aside "the first yield of your baking" for the Lord (17-21). Another repeats the law for strangers: 

> Numbers 15:15-16 - "One ordinance shall be both for you of the congregation, and also for the stranger that sojourneth with you, an ordinance for ever in your generations: as ye are, so shall the stranger be before the Lord. One law and one manner shall be for you, and for the stranger that sojourneth with you."

Some of these laws seem to contradict laws in Leviticus, so it is commonly thought that these laws came from a different time and place:

**1. Sacrifices for Community Sins**

When the entire community commits an unintentional sin, Numbers 15 and [Leviticus 4]({{ '/blog/leviticus/3-5' | relative_url }}) mandate completely different animal sacrifices for the exact same offense.

> Numbers 15:24 (NASB) - "then it shall be, if it is done unintentionally, without the knowledge of the congregation, that all the congregation shall offer one bull as a burnt offering, as a soothing aroma to the Lord, with its grain offering and its drink offering, according to the ordinance, and one male goat as a sin offering."

> Leviticus 4:14 (NASB) - "when the sin which they have committed becomes known, then the assembly shall offer a bull of the herd as a sin offering and bring it in front of the tent of meeting."

In Leviticus, the bull is the sin offering. In Numbers, the bull is shifted to a burnt offering, and a completely different animal (a goat) must be brought forward to act as the sin offering.

**2. Sacrifices for Individual Sins**

A parallel contradiction happens when an ordinary, individual citizen commits an accidental sin.

> Numbers 15:27 (NASB) - "Also, if one person sins unintentionally, then he shall offer a one-year-old female goat as a sin offering."

> Leviticus 4:27-28 (NASB) - "Now if anyone of the common people sins unintentionally by doing any of the things which the Lord has commanded not to be done, and becomes guilty, if his sin which he has committed is made known to him, then he shall bring as his offering a goat, a female without defect, for his sin which he has committed."

Leviticus gives the individual options for their sacrifice, whereas Numbers removes the option of the lamb entirely and restricts the ritual strictly to a goat.

**3. The Mandatory Inclusion of Wine and Grain**

In the first seven chapters of Leviticus, instructions for animal sacrifices (like burnt offerings or peace offerings) are laid out extensively without requiring accompanying side-offerings. However, Numbers 15:1-10 introduces a brand new, mandatory baseline: every animal sacrifice brought to the altar must be accompanied by a specific, scaled measurement of flour, oil, and wine.

For example:

> Numbers 15:6-7 (NASB) - "Or for a ram you shall prepare as a grain offering two-tenths of an ephah of fine flour mixed with a third of a hin of oil; and for the drink offering you shall offer a third of a hin of wine as a soothing aroma to the Lord."

Scholars view these contradictions as evidence that these laws in Numbers 15 were written by different priestly groups at different times in Israel's history. These laws represent a later revision or update to the religious code.

Other commentators argue that there is no true contradiction in the two sets of laws. They suggest Leviticus 4 deals with a sin of commission (doing something forbidden), wheres Numbers 15 applies to a sin of omission (failing to do something commanded), which accounts for the different animals required.

## A Court Case

Suddenly, in the midst of the laws, is a court case:

> Numbers 15:32-36 (NASB) - "Now while the sons of Israel were in the wilderness, they found a man gathering wood on the Sabbath day. And those who found him gathering wood brought him to Moses and Aaron, and to all the congregation; and they placed him in custody, because it had not been decided what should be done to him. Then the Lord said to Moses, 'The man must be put to death; all the congregation shall stone him with stones outside the camp.' So all the congregation brought him outside the camp and stoned him to death with stones, just as the Lord had commanded Moses."

At this time, it was not known how people should be punished for breaking the law of the Sabbath. Would the Lord punish them? Should the Israelites punish them? So Moses had the Israelites guard the guilty man while he asked for the Lord's decision.

It is a strange decision. Was this story meant to terrify us into keeping the Sabbath? Did the man deserve death just for gathering wood? Should a person be put to death for breaking the Sabbath? Usually, if the crime is serious, the punishment is severe; but if a crime is minor, the punishment is also minor.

> Exodus 31:14-16 - "Therefore you are to keep the Sabbath, for it is holy to you. Everyone who profanes it must be put to death; for whoever does any work on it, that person shall be cut off from among his people. For six days work may be done, but on the seventh day there is a Sabbath of complete rest, holy to the Lord; whoever does any work on the Sabbath day must be put to death. So the sons of Israel shall keep the Sabbath, to celebrate the Sabbath throughout their generations as a permanent covenant."

Even though the judgment came directly from the Lord, no punishment like this was ever again inflicted on an Israelite for breaking the law of the Sabbath. Modern scholars believe that we have only part of the story here; some part of the text is lost or missing.

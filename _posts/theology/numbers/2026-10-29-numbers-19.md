The Red Heifer

<br>

This chapter contains the law of the red heifer, which was judged by Jewish sages to be the most incomprehensible in the Torah. It became a classic example of a "statute", often understood as a law that has no reason, or at least none that we can understand. 

What is really odd is: What is the law doing here?

Surely this law belongs elsewhere. It is about a ritual of purification for one who had come into contact with the dead and now sought to enter the Tabernacle. It's place in the text should be in the book of Leviticus, which deals with purification rituals (see [Leviticus 12]({{ '/blog/leviticus/12-13' | relative_url}}) to [Leviticus 16]({{ '/blog/leviticus/16' | relative_url }})). It does not obviously belong in the book of Numbers, which deals with the second half of the Israelites' journey through the wilderness. 

We have just read about a serious revolt (or revolts) against the leadership of Moses and Aaron. What does this have to do with a law of purification?

## The Red Heifer

The Lord tells Moses and Aaron what must be doen when someone becomes unclean by touching or coming too close to a corpse, a human bone, or a grave. This happened often in ancient Israel. Since family and friends cared for the dying and the dead, most Israelites at one time had to touch, move, or bury a corpse. Yet they believed that contact with death made them unclean again. The Lord commands the ritual of the red heifer for this purpose.

A heifer is a young cow, one that has never given birth. The Lord instructs the Israelites to bring an "unblemished red heifer in which there is no defect" to Eleazar the priest. The red heifer must be sacrificed outside of the camp, with Eleazar watching. Eleazar must then dip his finger in the cow's blood and sprinkle the blood seven times in the direction of the Tent of Meeting. The dead heifer must be burned - adding cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet material (probably red yarn) to the fire. Eleazar and the person who helped him must then wash their clothes, bathe their bodies, and return to the camp. Because they were near the red heifer, they were unclean until evening. (2-8)

Someone else who was clean had to gather the ashes of the heifer and put them in a clean place outside of the camp. By touching the ashes, this person also became unclean until evening. To finish the preparations, the ashes of the heifer had to be mixed with fresh water. This mixture of water and ashes was considered to be purification from sin, and would remove impurity. (9-10)

The Lord commands: "The one who touches the body of any person will be unclean for seven days" (11). The water and ashes mixture for purification must be sprinkled on the unclean person on the third day and on the seventh day. Even the person who does the sprinkling becomes unclean until evening and has to bathe.

Why did touching the ashes or coming near the red heifer make a person unclean, while sprinkling the ashes on an unclean person would make that person clean? Why was this sacrifice done outside the camp or not on the altar of the Tabernacle? Why does the priest sprinkle blood seven times toward the Tent of Meeting? Why should this sacrifice be a heifer, and not a bull? Why is the color red important - the red skin of the cow, the blood, and the scarlet red yarn? These are just a few of the questions that have puzzled scholars and biblical commentators. 

Biblical law is not merely a set of rules whose only logic is that they are the will of God. The Law is rooted in history and cosmology. It reflects something other and larger than the law itself. Law is a way of putting right what we know, from history, to have been wrong. That is why the Torah includes both history (narrative) and law. God wants us to understand not only what to do, but also why. He wants us not just to obey, but also to reflect on and internalise the lessons of history.

Modern Bible scholars can help answer some of our questions. Thanks to their work, we know that in Mesopotamia, long before the time of Moses, magician-priests used this kind of ritual. The Israelite priests "adopted" these rituals, changing them slightly by taking out some of the "magic".

The sacrifice was done outside the camp, away from the Tabernacle, with a cow and not a bull - all of which shows that it was not a normal Israelite sacrifice. The red things (the cow's skin, the cedar wood, the blood, and the scarlet red yarn) all stood to represent blood. Blood was the stuff of life - losing it was bad, and being touched with blood that had been dedicated to God was good (good blood was used to ordain priests in [Exodus 29]({{ '/blog/exodus/28-30' | relative_url }})):

> Exodus 29:20-21 (NASB) - "And you shall slaughter the ram, and take some of its blood and put it on the lobe of Aaron’s right ear and on the lobes of his sons’ right ears, and on the thumbs of their right hands, and on the big toes of their right feet, and sprinkle the rest of the blood around on the altar. Then you shall take some of the blood that is on the altar and some of the anointing oil, and sprinkle it on Aaron and on his garments, and on his sons and on his sons’ garments with him; so he and his garments shall be consecrated, as well as his sons and his sons’ garments with him."

The cedar wood and hyssop gave off a pleasing aroma when they were burned, like the incense used in the Tabernacle.
